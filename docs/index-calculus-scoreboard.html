The calibrated index-calculus families below are scored against their generic-group floor and matched Pollard rho reference. On elliptic curves nothing measured end to end is below rho: the residual-walk thread's 500×-or-worse verdict on E(Fp3) is unchanged, and the three-regime ledger that heads the page puts the best whole-pipeline variant at 3.55× rho on a 23-bit prime-order curve, 16.0× on a 24-bit random binary curve and — after the collection and selection rounds of 2026-09-21 and 2026-09-22 — 1.17× on a 39-bit Koblitz subgroup, with no fitted exponent below the reference's. Two independent things moved those numbers and they must not be confused. The first two read lower than Round 4’s 3.63 and 21.9 for a reason that is not progress and is not counted as any: Round 5 re-seeded the walk rows, which moves a single row by up to a factor 1.8 in either direction at three repeats, and its own lever is exactly 1.000× on all three (see the panel below). That same re-seed took the Koblitz row from 30.3× to 26.1×. The 1.17× is separate and is progress: it is the collection and selection rounds measured end to end against a rho reference pooled over 224 targets of their own runs, so it is not the re-seeded row re-measured and the two references are not interchangeable. It is the closest anything here has come to rho on an elliptic curve and it is still above it; the rounds that produced it also drove the relation count to its counting floor, so that lever is spent rather than promising. Round 3 turns the general statement from a report into a boundary. The pair-table family has a derived shape law — a two-summand row pays F²/4t for its table and about c·#E/(2kF) for its relations, least at F = (c·#E·t/k)1/3 — and in this unit, where rho is a constant, that optimum is 0.75 r1/6 on a prime-order curve. A constant and an unbounded increasing function cross once, at r ≈ 22. Neither the base size, the two folds, the summand count nor the relation count changes that: for a row needing K1−a relations the optimum is r2/(3+a)−1/2, positive for every a < 1. So this family has no crossover to find at any size, and at sect163k1's size it sits about 107× the automorphism-aware walk it competes with. That r1/6 rise now has a measured crossing behind it rather than only a derivation: the tournament collector that sits strictly below rho on an eight-cell Koblitz panel reads 0.831 [0.772, 0.894] at r = 4.2×106 and 1.533 [1.238, 1.899] at r = 2.3×108, both bands clear of one in opposite directions, growing as r0.153 (below). Two cells fix a rate and no curvature, so that is agreement worth what a two-point rate is worth. That bounds the tabled family only; an algebraic decomposition oracle builds no such table and its asymptotics are the question this ladder cannot reach. It is also not blanket across problems: on hyperelliptic Jacobians at genus 3 and 4 index calculus measures below a real distinguished-point rho — 0.21 at N = 224 on the conservative accounting — with a fitted exponent matching the derived law. That is a toy-size result (p ≤ 251) whose reference needed correcting, not a statement about any deployed curve. The newer binary-field solver, the Crossbred decomposition oracle, the WDSat Riemann-Roch panel and the parameter-sweep pilots are explicitly uncalibrated: their component counts do not establish an end-to-end speedup or belong in that normalized claim.

The unit
S = ops / √n

Group operations per square root of the group order, so rho is flat at every size. Counted end to end across every phase — setup, relation finding, the decomposition oracle and linear algebra — with non-addition counters converted at ratios pinned in the repository rather than measured per run, because end-to-end speed is the measure of speed: a phase-only crossover is a diagnostic, not a win.

What was actually found

Three regimes now share one axis. A generic prime-field curve, a random binary curve and a Koblitz curve solve the same planted logarithm with every phase counted. A second round of engineering — the pair table folded by negation and, on Koblitz curves, by the Frobenius; targets drawn by an r-adding walk instead of two scalar multiplications each; two-summand rows wherever the cofactor classes admit them; a Koblitz base sized to the folded table — takes the best rows to 6.2×, 31× and 26× the counted rho, worth 1.3–2.9× per instance on the prime and binary ladders and 3–49× on the Koblitz one. Nothing crosses rho and no fitted exponent falls below the reference's, so every rung is engineering, not an advance. The counting ceiling is now computed exactly from the base's cofactor classes rather than argued: the index it corrects for is 1.9–2.2 on the trace-zero bases, 3.0 at n = 15 and 4.6 at n = 29.

A collision was being counted as a relation. A run that decomposes the same group element twice gets two rows with one factor-base part and different coefficients, and those pin the logarithm by themselves — a generic collision resolved through the factor base, arriving after about √r targets whatever the oracle costs. Measured: 24.4% of unguarded two-summand runs end that way, which is the predicted K²/(2rp) = 1/4, and 82% when walk segments share one jump table and merge as rho's walks do. Both target sources are now guarded, which costs 1.1–2.9× in S on the affected rows and moved 22 of the first round's 63 rows by up to 1.59×. The first-round figures on this page are the corrected ones (research/notes/index-calculus/RESEARCH_IC_BOUNDARY_LEDGER.md §10.2).

The walk hypothesis is closed. Collision search over partial decompositions cannot beat independent sampling, because the relation yield is bounded by a counting argument. Every lever tried left the ratio to that floor between 1.00 and 1.07.

Published constants are soft. The three-unknown solve lost a factor of 5.5 to ordinary linear algebra, worth fifteen bits of crossover. If the same slack exists elsewhere, quoted crossover sizes are optimistic.

The n4/9 is real. Measured 0.44 against the predicted 0.444, end to end, every run recovering the planted logarithm.

The 2/3 is exact too. Pair-only decompositions were predicted to need n2/3 residuals at k = 3; measured 0.666 over four sizes, the cleanest confirmation of a predicted exponent on this board.

One diagnosis on this board was wrong. The residuals no Macaulay degree closes were recorded as having solutions at infinity, fixable by saturation. Measured, the ideal is zero-dimensional, the affine variety carries one F_p-point where a positive- dimensional component would carry about p, and a generic change of coordinates rescued none of 28. It is the staircase border meeting the degree cut — a truncation artefact, so the fix is a border basis, not saturation.

Where the boundaries bite

The plain method never wins. Relations cost n1/3 and its linear algebra n0.68, so cost bottoms out near 200× rho around 250 and rises after. No reduction in the solve constant changes that.

The large-prime variant wins too late. Its score falls as n−1/18, so closing the measured factor of 1,987 takes roughly two hundred doublings of the group order.

The loose end is closed, and the missing piece does not fit. This panel said row weight doubling pushes the large-prime linear algebra to n0.56 and that “capping the merge level is the missing piece”. The cap was built and measured over six seeds, paired so each seed is its own control — the fitted exponent moves by 0.04 between seeds, which is the size of the whole effect, and a two-seed version of this comparison said the opposite of the truth. At k = 12 the solve exponent falls 0.045 ± 0.017 (2.6σ, five seeds of six) and row weight at p = 2083 drops 20.93 → 11.89, exactly as intended — and the eliminator's own work rises 0.043 ± 0.030, cancelling it, so the linear algebra as a whole moves −0.009 ± 0.019, which is nothing. End to end the cap is worse, +0.051 ± 0.031 on five seeds of six. Its cost is not the 0.3–2% of relations it discards but the full relations that never form: yield per residual falls to 0.64× at the two largest sizes against 0.95× at the smallest, and a yield loss that grows in n is a residual exponent that rises. Mean merge depth is flat across the ladder, so chains are not lengthening — the tail past the cap thickens. Class engineering, negative; S is untouched, as the pre-registration said in advance it would be (experiments/22_gaudry_merge_cap_uncapped.json, …_k12.json; §§11.10–11.11).

And the exponent it was built to fix was never pinned. The n0.56 above is a two-seed figure quoted without an error bar. Six seeds give 0.516 ± 0.027 — still 2.7σ above 4/9, so the concern was real — and 0.56 sits inside a two-seed ±0.046. Meanwhile the end-to-end exponent is 0.424 ± 0.016, consistent with 4/9: the large-prime variant does reach n4/9 over this ladder, because its linear algebra is about 2% of the cost. The higher linear-algebra exponent bites only near 298, and capping the merge level is not how to meet it there.

A bigger field is not a lever. Base-changing ECC2K-130 to F2131e leaves the target group and its 262 automorphisms untouched, so the reference stays 260.81 at every e. Because 131 is prime, every subfield and every Frobenius-stable subspace of that field is either of dimension ≥ 130 — a 2131 factor base, 270.19× rho at the cheapest e and rising with e — or contained in F2e, where the factor base lies inside the subgroup E(F2e) and no relation exists at any cost. Checked for e = 1…16; embedding degree > 107 closes the transfer route (experiments/ecc2k130_extension_field_boundary.json).

A bigger genus is not a lever either. A cover over F2131 is priced by that field, so the cheapest one, genus 2, costs 2131 — 270.19× rho, the same row as the bigger field. Over F2, the only proper subfield, index calculus would be cheap: genus 130 would cost 237.17, 223.64× below the reference. But because 2 is a primitive root mod 131, the GHS genus available to every curve over F2131 is 1, 2129 or 2130, and at genus 1 the transfer is exactly the zero map on the target subgroup. The window that would pay is genus 130 to between 290 and 300; the gap to the nearest available genus is 2120.77 (experiments/ecc2k130_hyperelliptic_cover_boundary.json).

Homogeneous relations do not exist to be found. Complete three-point sweeps over the weight-two support and its variants, every pair of every base, found 0 relations on the 4,262-point weight-two support (2−95.4 predicted) and 0 on its 1,090-point σ-stable subset (2−101.3). The one hit, on the 4,336-point weight-at-most-two support, is degenerate. Four-point, orbit-grouped: eight normal bases swept to completion, 1,020,114 pair-orbits (227.0 signed pairs), 490 candidates, 0 certified against an expectation of 2−79.4. All 490 candidates are instances of σ2 + σ + 2 = 0 — exactly 2 per lifting orbit — so the search recovers the endomorphism ring’s own identity and nothing else. Every yield matches the counting prediction; no support evades the floor (research/ecc2k130_relations/results/summary.json, four_point_orbit_summary.json).

Frobenius is a real saving and not a lever. On a normal basis the weight-two support is closed under σ, so the stored side of the meet-in-the-middle holds one canonical class per orbit and a 131 × T-point support carries only T unknowns. Applied at every m it is worth 216.1–218.4, more at larger m, so it does not change which m is cheapest — it changes the margin (four_point_orbit_boundary.json).

Extended past m = 8 the homogeneous table crosses rho, and that is the trap. Continuing the quotiented self-consistent accounting, it goes sub-reference at m = 18 and reaches 2−4.05 at m = 28. Those rows price relations among base points alone, which give one linear equation among unknown logarithms and determine logP(Q) not at all.

Parity is below the floor, so it is unreachable rather than unreached. Balancing the build against the streamed side collapses every configuration to total = 2√(K₁ · U · C(n,s) · r) with K₁ = 0.265358. U ≥ 2 (one orbit, plus d) and C(n,s) ≥ n ≥ 2, so every configuration costs at least 265.54 whatever the support size, relation length, split or table layout. The reference is 260.81: parity sits 2-4.73 below the floor, and closing it would need the constant to fall 26.6× with nothing left to take it from. Structurally, the 262 automorphisms enter rho’s constant inside its square root, while the Frobenius quotient divides index calculus’s unknown count and table size — both real, worth 214 together, and neither touching the square root a two-list collision search over a space of size r must pay. Moving it needs a decomposition oracle cheaper than that square root, not a better constant.

Decomposing the points is not the hard part; finding the decomposition is. At the challenge parameters a target of ECC2K-130 does have factor-base decompositions — the yield law C(|F|, m)/#E holds to within 1.09× over twelve toy cells (one small-base outlier at 0.57× aside), it needs only dim V ≥ 45 at m = 3, the cofactor-4 class admits every m ≥ 2 measured on the curve itself, and certified m = 2, 3, 4 witnesses with S4 = 0 are in the artefact. Finding one is what costs: relations × targets × oracle = m · 2131 independently of the factor-base dimension, so the best implicit-base variant is 2132.58, 2+71.77× rho, and no table rescues it — at 230 through 270 entries plain BSGS on the same memory is cheaper than the best decomposition cell, and rho is cheaper than both while storing nothing. Stated as a demand on the oracle: it would have to beat exhaustive search over its own candidate set by 270.19 + log₂ m, a number that does not move with m or with l because it is the product law (experiments/ecc2k130_point_decomposition.json).

A trace-zero factor base yields twice, and that is worth exactly one bit. A base whose abscissae lie in ker Tr sits inside 2E, an index-2 subgroup, so every sum of its points stays there — and the target subgroup is inside it too. The sums are spread over #E/2, not #E, so a target is hit twice as often as C(|F|, m)/#E says. Measured exhaustively over every target of the order-r subgroup at n = 13, 17, 19: the generic base occupies 0.98–1.00 × #E and the trace-zero base 0.49–0.50 × #E, a yield factor of 1.97–2.04. Deciding is localising, for any detector that takes its target as input. A hypothetical decomposition oracle that answers only yes/no for the whole factor base looks far weaker than one that hands over the summands. It is not. Let R decompose; for each candidate P in the base draw a base point Q of the same cofactor class and ask the detector about R − P + Q — a class match keeps the query inside the target subgroup, so even a subgroup-only detector suffices. If P is a summand the query point is literally an m-sum of base points and the answer is yes by construction; if it is not, the point is generic and the answer is yes with probability λ. Two independent Q per candidate and the summands fall out of 2|F| whole-base queries, with no sub-base query anywhere. The group operations that build them cost k · 22l against the m · 22l the linear algebra already pays, so they are absorbed.

Measured at n = 13, 17, 19 over eight rungs, 64 built targets each, zero sub-base queries on every one. The two ways it misfires are collisions of Q with a summand or its negative — a miss at rate Θ(m/|F|) per draw, a false positive at Θ(m/|F|) + λ — and the measured rates stay inside 0.22–0.87 of those predictions across a sixteenfold range of m/|F| and of λ, with no third error term. Read off at the free-oracle optimum for m = 4 (l = 26.70, m/|F| = 2−24.70): localisation fails on one target in 220.66, losing 26.04 of the 226.70 relations needed.

So there is one free-oracle floor, not two: 256.76 at m = 4, under the 260.81 reference from m = 4 onward, monotonically, with no table. F5 for the Koblitz Gröbner stage: the criterion prunes nothing at degree 3, the inheritance it implies cuts the stage 6.28× engineering

Two F5 ideas applied to the matrix-F4 splitting solver behind the Koblitz decomposition oracle. The F5 criterion, stated correctly for the Boolean ring (the textbook F_2 rule “prune t·f_i for t ∈ LM⟨f_1..f_i⟩” is unsound there, because x₁·(x₁x₂+x₁+x₂) = x₁; the Frobenius syzygies must enter as polynomials), removes nothing from the quadratic degree-3 matrices the oracle solves — the trivial syzygies live at degree 4 — and 0–12% of rows where linear equations appear: 1.001× net of its own cost. Inherited matrix-F4 applies F5’s idea to the axis the solver actually iterates along: substitution is a ring homomorphism, so a child’s Macaulay row space is the image of its parent’s reduced rows, and only the ≈ 3/n of them whose pivot contains the assigned variable need re-reducing. No matrix is built below the root. It is the default engine on quadratic systems; on the chained cubic m ≥ 3 systems it loses (0.60×: their 40%-rank-deficient matrices make the reduced rows dense) and the default routes them to the from-scratch engine.
