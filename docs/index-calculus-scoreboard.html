Index Calculus Scoreboard

The calibrated index-calculus families below are scored against their generic-group floor and matched Pollard rho reference. On elliptic curves nothing measured end to end is below rho: the residual-walk thread's 500×-or-worse verdict on E(Fp3) is unchanged, and the three-regime ledger that heads the page puts the best whole-pipeline variant at 3.55× rho on a 23-bit prime-order curve, 16.0× on a 24-bit random binary curve and — after the collection and selection rounds of 2026-09-21 and 2026-09-22 — 1.17× on a 39-bit Koblitz subgroup, with no fitted exponent below the reference's. Two independent things moved those numbers and they must not be confused. The first two read lower than Round 4’s 3.63 and 21.9 for a reason that is not progress and is not counted as any: Round 5 re-seeded the walk rows, which moves a single row by up to a factor 1.8 in either direction at three repeats, and its own lever is exactly 1.000× on all three (see the panel below). That same re-seed took the Koblitz row from 30.3× to 26.1×. The 1.17× is separate and is progress: it is the collection and selection rounds measured end to end against a rho reference pooled over 224 targets of their own runs, so it is not the re-seeded row re-measured and the two references are not interchangeable. It is the closest anything here has come to rho on an elliptic curve and it is still above it; the rounds that produced it also drove the relation count to its counting floor, so that lever is spent rather than promising. Round 3 turns the general statement from a report into a boundary. The pair-table family has a derived shape law — a two-summand row pays F²/4t for its table and about c·#E/(2kF) for its relations, least at F = (c·#E·t/k)1/3 — and in this unit, where rho is a constant, that optimum is 0.75 r1/6 on a prime-order curve. A constant and an unbounded increasing function cross once, at r ≈ 22. Neither the base size, the two folds, the summand count nor the relation count changes that: for a row needing K1−a relations the optimum is r2/(3+a)−1/2, positive for every a < 1. So this family has no crossover to find at any size, and at sect163k1's size it sits about 107× the automorphism-aware walk it competes with. That r1/6 rise now has a measured crossing behind it rather than only a derivation: the tournament collector that sits strictly below rho on an eight-cell Koblitz panel reads 0.831 [0.772, 0.894] at r = 4.2×106 and 1.533 [1.238, 1.899] at r = 2.3×108, both bands clear of one in opposite directions, and beyond the crossing the ratio grows at r[0.13, 0.19] across three certified cells and 1100× in r (below). The derived exponent lies inside that range — but at a factor base measurably not where the same derivation puts it, so the agreement is not yet confirmation of the model. That bounds the tabled family only; an algebraic decomposition oracle builds no such table and its asymptotics are the question this ladder cannot reach. It is also not blanket across problems: on hyperelliptic Jacobians at genus 3 and 4 index calculus measures below a real distinguished-point rho — 0.21 at N = 224 on the conservative accounting — with a fitted exponent matching the derived law. That is a toy-size result (p ≤ 251) whose reference needed correcting, not a statement about any deployed curve. The newer binary-field solver, the Crossbred decomposition oracle, the WDSat Riemann-Roch panel and the parameter-sweep pilots are explicitly uncalibrated: their component counts do not establish an end-to-end speedup or belong in that normalized claim.

The unit
S = ops / √n

Group operations per square root of the group order, so rho is flat at every size. Counted end to end across every phase — setup, relation finding, the decomposition oracle and linear algebra — with non-addition counters converted at ratios pinned in the repository rather than measured per run, because end-to-end speed is the measure of speed: a phase-only crossover is a diagnostic, not a win.

What was actually found

Three regimes now share one axis. A generic prime-field curve, a random binary curve and a Koblitz curve solve the same planted logarithm with every phase counted. A second round of engineering — the pair table folded by negation and, on Koblitz curves, by the Frobenius; targets drawn by an r-adding walk instead of two scalar multiplications each; two-summand rows wherever the cofactor classes admit them; a Koblitz base sized to the folded table — takes the best rows to 6.2×, 31× and 26× the counted rho, worth 1.3–2.9× per instance on the prime and binary ladders and 3–49× on the Koblitz one. Nothing crosses rho and no fitted exponent falls below the reference's, so every rung is engineering, not an advance. The counting ceiling is now computed exactly from the base's cofactor classes rather than argued: the index it corrects for is 1.9–2.2 on the trace-zero bases, 3.0 at n = 15 and 4.6 at n = 29.

A collision was being counted as a relation. A run that decomposes the same group element twice gets two rows with one factor-base part and different coefficients, and those pin the logarithm by themselves — a generic collision resolved through the factor base, arriving after about √r targets whatever the oracle costs. Measured: 24.4% of unguarded two-summand runs end that way, which is the predicted K²/(2rp) = 1/4, and 82% when walk segments share one jump table and merge as rho's walks do. Both target sources are now guarded, which costs 1.1–2.9× in S on the affected rows and moved 22 of the first round's 63 rows by up to 1.59×. The first-round figures on this page are the corrected ones (research/notes/index-calculus/RESEARCH_IC_BOUNDARY_LEDGER.md §10.2).

The walk hypothesis is closed. Collision search over partial decompositions cannot beat independent sampling, because the relation yield is bounded by a counting argument. Every lever tried left the ratio to that floor between 1.00 and 1.07.

Published constants are soft. The three-unknown solve lost a factor of 5.5 to ordinary linear algebra, worth fifteen bits of crossover. If the same slack exists elsewhere, quoted crossover sizes are optimistic.

The n4/9 is real. Measured 0.44 against the predicted 0.444, end to end, every run recovering the planted logarithm.

The 2/3 is exact too. Pair-only decompositions were predicted to need n2/3 residuals at k = 3; measured 0.666 over four sizes, the cleanest confirmation of a predicted exponent on this board.

One diagnosis on this board was wrong. The residuals no Macaulay degree closes were recorded as having solutions at infinity, fixable by saturation. Measured, the ideal is zero-dimensional, the affine variety carries one F_p-point where a positive- dimensional component would carry about p, and a generic change of coordinates rescued none of 28. It is the staircase border meeting the degree cut — a truncation artefact, so the fix is a border basis, not saturation. The fixed-staircase border basis built in §11.14 does not reach them: their staircase is not the generic one (dimension 61 at degree 10), so no per-curve plan serves them. Whether Mourrain's full iteration would is untested.

Where the boundaries bite

The plain method never wins. Relations cost n1/3 and its linear algebra n0.68, so cost bottoms out near 200× rho around 250 and rises after. No reduction in the solve constant changes that.

The large-prime variant wins too late. Its score falls as n−1/18, so closing the measured factor of 1,987 takes roughly two hundred doublings of the group order.

The loose end is closed, and the missing piece does not fit. This panel said row weight doubling pushes the large-prime linear algebra to n0.56 and that “capping the merge level is the missing piece”. The cap was built and measured over six seeds, paired so each seed is its own control — the fitted exponent moves by 0.04 between seeds, which is the size of the whole effect, and a two-seed version of this comparison said the opposite of the truth. At k = 12 the solve exponent falls 0.045 ± 0.017 (2.6σ, five seeds of six) and row weight at p = 2083 drops 20.93 → 11.89, exactly as intended — and the eliminator's own work rises 0.043 ± 0.030, cancelling it, so the linear algebra as a whole moves −0.009 ± 0.019, which is nothing. End to end the cap is worse, +0.051 ± 0.031 on five seeds of six. Its cost is not the 0.3–2% of relations it discards but the full relations that never form: yield per residual falls to 0.64× at the two largest sizes against 0.95× at the smallest, and a yield loss that grows in n is a residual exponent that rises. Mean merge depth is flat across the ladder, so chains are not lengthening — the tail past the cap thickens. Class engineering, negative; S is untouched, as the pre-registration said in advance it would be (experiments/22_gaudry_merge_cap_uncapped.json, …_k12.json; §§11.10–11.11).

And the exponent it was built to fix was never pinned. The n0.56 above is a two-seed figure quoted without an error bar. Six seeds give 0.516 ± 0.027 — still 2.7σ above 4/9, so the concern was real — and 0.56 sits inside a two-seed ±0.046. Meanwhile the end-to-end exponent is 0.424 ± 0.016, consistent with 4/9: the large-prime variant does reach n4/9 over this ladder, because its linear algebra is about 2% of the cost. The higher linear-algebra exponent bites only near 298, and capping the merge level is not how to meet it there.

A bigger field is not a lever. Base-changing ECC2K-130 to F2131e leaves the target group and its 262 automorphisms untouched, so the reference stays 260.81 at every e. Because 131 is prime, every subfield and every Frobenius-stable subspace of that field is either of dimension ≥ 130 — a 2131 factor base, 270.19× rho at the cheapest e and rising with e — or contained in F2e, where the factor base lies inside the subgroup E(F2e) and no relation exists at any cost. Checked for e = 1…16; embedding degree > 107 closes the transfer route (experiments/ecc2k130_extension_field_boundary.json).

A bigger genus is not a lever either. A cover over F2131 is priced by that field, so the cheapest one, genus 2, costs 2131 — 270.19× rho, the same row as the bigger field. Over F2, the only proper subfield, index calculus would be cheap: genus 130 would cost 237.17, 223.64× below the reference. But because 2 is a primitive root mod 131, the GHS genus available to every curve over F2131 is 1, 2129 or 2130, and at genus 1 the transfer is exactly the zero map on the target subgroup. The window that would pay is genus 130 to between 290 and 300; the gap to the nearest available genus is 2120.77 (experiments/ecc2k130_hyperelliptic_cover_boundary.json).

Homogeneous relations do not exist to be found. Complete three-point sweeps over the weight-two support and its variants, every pair of every base, found 0 relations on the 4,262-point weight-two support (2−95.4 predicted) and 0 on its 1,090-point σ-stable subset (2−101.3). The one hit, on the 4,336-point weight-at-most-two support, is degenerate. Four-point, orbit-grouped: eight normal bases swept to completion, 1,020,114 pair-orbits (227.0 signed pairs), 490 candidates, 0 certified against an expectation of 2−79.4. All 490 candidates are instances of σ2 + σ + 2 = 0 — exactly 2 per lifting orbit — so the search recovers the endomorphism ring’s own identity and nothing else. Every yield matches the counting prediction; no support evades the floor (research/ecc2k130_relations/results/summary.json, four_point_orbit_summary.json).

Frobenius is a real saving and not a lever. On a normal basis the weight-two support is closed under σ, so the stored side of the meet-in-the-middle holds one canonical class per orbit and a 131 × T-point support carries only T unknowns. Applied at every m it is worth 216.1–218.4, more at larger m, so it does not change which m is cheapest — it changes the margin (four_point_orbit_boundary.json).

Deciding is localising, for any detector that takes its target as input. A hypothetical decomposition oracle that answers only yes/no for the whole factor base looks far weaker than one that hands over the summands. It is not. Let R decompose; for each candidate P in the base draw a base point Q of the same cofactor class and ask the detector about R − P + Q — a class match keeps the query inside the target subgroup, so even a subgroup-only detector suffices. If P is a summand the query point is literally an m-sum of base points and the answer is yes by construction; if it is not, the point is generic and the answer is yes with probability λ. Two independent Q per candidate and the summands fall out of 2|F| whole-base queries, with no sub-base query anywhere. The group operations that build them cost k · 22l against the m · 22l the linear algebra already pays, so they are absorbed.

Measured at n = 13, 17, 19 over eight rungs, 64 built targets each, zero sub-base queries on every one. The two ways it misfires are collisions of Q with a summand or its negative — a miss at rate Θ(m/|F|) per draw, a false positive at Θ(m/|F|) + λ — and the measured rates stay inside 0.22–0.87 of those predictions across a sixteenfold range of m/|F| and of λ, with no third error term. Read off at the free-oracle optimum for m = 4 (l = 26.70, m/|F| = 2−24.70): localisation fails on one target in 220.66, losing 26.04 of the 226.70 relations needed.

So there is one free-oracle floor, not two: 256.76 at m = 4, under the 260.81 reference from m = 4 onward, monotonically, with no table. The only detector this fails against is one restricted to a fixed family of targets that refuses R − P + Q — it would have to extract witnesses by meet-in-the-middle, bottoming at 256.93 with a 253.82-entry table — and no proposed detector has that shape: a resultant-vanishing test, a trace condition, a fixed-degree Gröbner refutation and the Nagao/Riemann–Roch coefficient search are all algorithms on the target's coordinates. Two earlier revisions of this panel priced that distinction as the curve's protective margin, first at 237.68 and then at 211–224. The margin is not there; class accounting both times (experiments/ecc2k130_point_decomposition.json, experiments/ecc2k130_decomposition_targets.json).

What would count next

The candidate this panel named has been measured. Decomposing into two base points instead of three does carry orders of magnitude: the per-residual constant falls 580×, from 0.9 million field multiplications to 1,513. It also lands far above rho, at 1,037× at 33 bits, so by this ledger's own test the fixed-degree direction is quantitatively closed at toy scale.

What is left is not a cheaper oracle. Two routes were measured and both are shut: a Gröbner solve on the descended system for fixed X₁ has a deciding degree that grows as ℓ/2, and the multilinear block structure of these systems cannot be used as a Macaulay shift restriction at all, because the only shift that preserves multilinearity is the trivial one. The open direction is a border basis in place of the fixed-degree Macaulay cut, and a merge-level cap for the large-prime linear algebra. Both were built or bounded, and neither helps. The merge-level cap is measured in §11.11: it lowers the solve exponent 0.045 ± 0.017 and the eliminator cancels it, leaving the method +0.051 ± 0.031 worse end to end. The border basis was bounded in §11.13 at 1.06× and then written (§11.14), which showed the bound charged work to the wrong rows. The normal forms read 149 of the 222 pivot rows, but those were reduced through almost all the rest: the rows they depend on are 202 of 222 at every size, the work outside them is 1.1% of the elimination, 0.19% of C₃ (5.6% as §11.13 counted it), and the ceiling is 1.002× (accounting). The solver gets 98.9% of that on the residuals it solves; the 0.3–2.4% whose staircase differs from the per-curve plan cost it a wasted attempt, so it loses 0.08–0.14% at p = 271 and gains up to 0.14% by p = 2083 — 1.0009× pooled end to end, zero mismatches over 20,146 cross-checked residuals. It stays off by default. What §11.5 already does — forward elimination plus memoised back-substitution restricted to the monomials actually reached — is a border-basis computation in all but name, which is why nothing was left. Class engineering for both, and neither is a finding.

What would count now is a change in the closing rate, not in a constant (§11.15). At k = 3 every constant lever is spent, and the obstacle is structural: the linear algebra over |F| ∝ n1/3 unknowns costs n2/3, above rho's n1/2, and the large-prime cure closes only as n−1/18. The one lever left is the extension degree. At k = 4 the linear algebra is |F|2 ∝ n1/2, the same exponent as rho, and the relation phase grows as n1/4·C₄, so the plain method's ratio to rho tends to a constant instead of growing. That constant is the sparse solver's cost against rho's and does not depend on the solve; C₄, the cost of one S₅ solve over Fp⁴, sets only where the relation phase stops dominating.

Derived before building (§11.16, from §11.7's measured Wiedemann constant and Fp⁴ operation counts): the constant is 0.34–0.63× rho, so a plain k = 4 method would eventually beat rho by 1.6–3× and no more (measured below). This note's own solver bounds C₄ ≥ 7.1·1010 from the characteristic polynomial of a 4,096-dimensional multiplication matrix alone, which put the handover at n ≈ 2133–2137, extrapolated on the exponents n1/4 and n1/2, and a measured C₄ in this design could only move it later. Decomposing into three points instead (Joux–Vitse) sits near 36× rho at every size.

Then measured (§11.17, four residuals at p = 269, each solve cross-checked against a meet-in-the-middle oracle, the constructed one returning its planted decomposition): the S₅ system has the predicted dimension 4,096, an Fp⁴ addition costs the derived 97 multiplications, and C₄ = 1.21·1012, 17× the floor. The same solver on generic systems of the same degrees predicts it within 4%, so the summation polynomial is no cheaper than any four octics. The handover moves to n ≈ 2149–2153 (extrapolated over the derived constant; narrowed below), with the relation phase at 4.3·108× rho at 32 bits — still about eighty doublings before the k = 3 crossover. A stage diagnostic: no end-to-end S exists for k = 4, and none is claimed.

Then the constant, measured (§11.19, registered in §11.18: eight prime-order curves over Fp⁴ at p = 269–1033, n ≈ 232–240, relations from a meet-in-the-middle oracle, the linear algebra run to a verified logarithm on every curve, rho run 16 times on each, all 128 verified): the linear algebra costs r∞ = 0.518 ± 0.031× rho, flat in n (fitted exponent −0.021 ± 0.017), inside the derived band. So a plain k = 4 method would beat rho by 1.9× and no more. The one derived input that was off was the filtering fraction, 0.897 measured against the 0.73 borrowed from k = 3, and at the measured value the derived formula gives 0.510. The handover narrows to n ≈ 2151.1 (2150.8–2151.5), extrapolated on the same exponents and C₄. Class: a stage diagnostic. It prices the linear algebra against rho, while the stand-in relation phase at these sizes is 1,054–4,988× rho and the S₅ one 4.3·108×. The registered outcome was confirmed.

Nothing here threatens a deployed curve, and nothing is claimed to. The point is to locate the crossovers by measurement rather than extrapolation.

F5 for the Koblitz Gröbner stage: the criterion prunes nothing at degree 3; inheriting the parent’s reduced basis, splitting on the variable that displaces the fewest of its rows, and rewriting only the rows a level touches cuts the stage 21× engineering

Two F5 ideas applied to the matrix-F4 splitting solver behind the Koblitz decomposition oracle. The F5 criterion, stated correctly for the Boolean ring (the textbook F_2 rule “prune t·f_i for t ∈ LM⟨f_1..f_i⟩” is unsound there, because x₁·(x₁x₂+x₁+x₂) = x₁; the Frobenius syzygies must enter as polynomials), removes nothing from the quadratic degree-3 matrices the oracle solves — the trivial syzygies live at degree 4 — and 0–12% of rows where linear equations appear: 1.001× net of its own cost. Inherited matrix-F4 applies F5’s idea to the axis the solver actually iterates along: substitution is a ring homomorphism, so a child’s Macaulay row space is the image of its parent’s reduced rows, and only the rows whose pivot contains the assigned variable need re-reducing. No matrix is built below the root. Two measured facts then set the constant. Which variable decides how many rows are displaced: a pivot is a row’s largest DegRevLex monomial, so the historical rule — split on the lowest-indexed free variable, the largest in the order — picks the variable most pivots contain and displaces half the basis per level deep in the tree; splitting on the smallest free variable halves the engine’s work and turns its one loss (the chained cubic systems, 0.60×) into a win. And three quarters of the rows a level keeps are never touched at that level, so a kept row is shared by reference and rewritten into a newer layout only when something needs it, through the composition of every step since, charged once.

Boundary: the m·2131 candidate-tuple count of the ECC2K-130 decomposition note — no term for how a Macaulay matrix is reduced or how many are built, so the ratio to it is flat by construction. Reference: SolverEngine::MatrixF4 { max_degree: 3 } splitting on the lowest free variable, the pre-round default, on the same binary (KIC_F4_INHERIT=0).
