01 / BELOW THE RHO REFERENCE

The index-calculus pipeline that beats matched rho

Complete cold single-target Koblitz ECDLP recovery, one public target per process, against a Pollard rho baseline built from the same source, on the same curve, in the same process.

beats_rho_strict · every cell

This is a different thread from the summation-polynomial measurements further down the page, and it is the one measured directly against the rho reference rather than against an internal baseline. In the repository's own classification it is still engineering, not an advance: S fell against the reference, while the ratio to the counting floor did not move — nothing here does something a generic algorithm cannot. The unit is the repository's standard S = total operations / √r for a group of prime order r, which makes Pollard rho a flat reference and turns “is this better than rho” into reading one column. The operations are fixed-compiler Valgrind amd64 user-space instruction reads, charged for the whole job: curve construction, factor-base construction and its tables, failed probes, relation collection, the scalar-field solve, every column-logarithm certification, the descent, and the final general-arithmetic check of the recovered scalar.

That figure is the recipe's floor, not the built size: SubgroupOrbits{seed, points} draws representatives a batch at a time and stops at the first base that reaches the floor, and each representative contributes a whole signed Frobenius orbit of 2n points. On these cells that is a batch of seven or eight orbits — which is also the relation-column count K, since the columns are the orbits, |F| / 2n. The rho arm is checked against its own model in every round: on these cells its walk lands within 0.63–1.14× of √(πr/2A) with A = 2n and at most one restart, so the reference is a healthy rho exploiting the same automorphisms, not a broken one.

What the job actually does

  1. Construct the curve and lift the target

    One single-word Koblitz curve per job: point count, generator search, the Frobenius eigenvalue λ as a root of λ² − tλ + 2 (mod r) by one-word Tonelli–Shanks, and the public target lifted from its abscissa. Both arms pay this, and it is inside every ratio above.

  2. Build the factor base from the prime-order subgroup

    SubgroupOrbits{seed, points}: draw a random abscissa, lift it to P, keep [h]P for the cofactor h. Close under Frobenius and negation. See the next section for why this shape, and not a linearised-polynomial subspace, is what makes the rate right.

  3. Fold as many pair-sum rows as pay for themselves

    The meet-in-the-middle table stores one witness per Frobenius orbit of each pair sum, under a normal-basis rotation key. A cost rule decides how many rows to build at all — on these cells one row, or two on n23a0, against seven or eight before the rule existed.

  4. Walk probes and decompose into three summands

    Probes are walked, Rt+1 = Rt + S, rather than drawn with a scalar multiplication each. Each probe is tested for R = Pi + Pj + Pk against the folded table; the search stops at the first witness. Repetition is allowed, direct relations are not.

  5. Solve the scalar system on signed Frobenius orbits

    One column per signed Frobenius orbit after cofactor projection, so the |F| unknowns collapse to ≈ |F| / 2n. Each relation is a row over those columns: summand i contributes its known λk weight to its orbit's column, and the right-hand side is h·a. Duplicate rows are detected and dropped; collection continues until the rows determine every column, and the echelon solve then runs in single-word arithmetic mod r.

  6. Certify every column, then descend

    Every column logarithm is re-checked in the group as [ℓo]G = [h]Co, batched behind one field inversion per bit. The target is then recovered by walking probes [a]G + [b]Q with a fixed stride until one decomposes; that single descent relation [a]G + [b]Q = Σ Pi gives the scalar, and it is written into the receipt.

  7. Verify the scalar in general arithmetic

    [d]G = Q, in the general binary-field implementation rather than the fast one, before anything is returned. Every one of the 1,440 trials in the round carries certified_descents = 1 and an independently audited receipt.

Where the margin came from, round by round

The ratio moved thirteen times, and not always for algorithmic reasons. Rounds 0009–0013 changed only shared or implementation-level work, and round 0013 changed it in rho's favour: putting every scalar product in López–Dahab coordinates with one inversion per product halved rho's own job, because rho's setup is scalar products. The 0.77 figure is the one to quote, because it is the one measured against a rho that is not paying for an affine double-and-add.

02 / FACTOR BASE

Choosing the base by what the pipeline pays for

Two decisions: which points are in it, and how many columns they cost.

shape, not size

The run cost is trials × (cost per trial) + linear algebra, and the expected trial count is (U + 1 + extra) / pm, where U is the number of relation columns and pm the fraction of subgroup points that decompose into m base points. Neither |F| nor the dimension is the objective; U / pm is.

expected trials = (U + 1 + extra) / p_m columns U ≈ |F| / 2n p_m ≈ |F|^m / (m! · r)

Frobenius invariance is what makes the columns cheap

On a Koblitz curve Ka : y² + xy = x³ + a x² + 1 the squaring Frobenius π(x,y) = (x², y²) is an endomorphism, acting on the prime-order subgroup as multiplication by λ. If the base is π-invariant then logG πk(Q) = λk logG Q, so every orbit is one unknown instead of n:

What invariance buys, by stage
StageClassicalFrobenius-invariantSaving
Relations needed|F||F| / nn
Matrix, both dimensions|F||F| / nn²
Decomposition searchm! copies of each relationordered tuples onlym!

Merging P with −P and projecting by the cofactor takes this further, to ≈ |F| / 2n signed orbits. Measured on the toys, |F| / #orbits is 7.9× at n = 9, 10.9× at n = 11 and 13.0× at n = 13 in relations, and its square in linear algebra — against rho's √(2n) from the same structure, which is 4.2×, 4.7× and 5.1×. Index calculus gains more from the subfield structure than rho does.

The linear base was the wrong shape

Every base family in this pipeline used to be linear: an invariant subspace {P : Fj(x(P)) = 0} built from the linearised polynomial of a factor of xn − 1, a union of Frobenius translates of one, or a subset. That is an inheritance from the algebraic oracle — Semaev's polynomials and the Weil restriction are written over a subspace. A meet-in-the-middle oracle never looks at the base's structure, and the structure is expensive. At n = 41, over 3,000 random subgroup targets at m = 3:

How often a random subgroup target is a sum of three base points

Two effects, the second larger. Linearity: the sumset of a structured set is far from uniform, so its sums hit a given target less often than three random points would. The cofactor: a relation asks for a target of ⟨G⟩ to be a sum of base points, and a point outside ⟨G⟩ can only sum into it when the cofactor parts cancel, so a base drawn from the whole curve spends most of its sums in the wrong coset. The column count is unchanged either way, so the linear algebra does not pay for the improvement.

Drawing a subgroup base without knowing any logarithm

build_subgroup_orbit_factor_base(kc, seed, points) draws a random abscissa, lifts it to a point P, and keeps [h]P. That lands in ⟨G⟩ for every P, so no draw is wasted, and knowing P says nothing about log [h]P. The first version instead tested [r]P = O and rejected failures — also logarithm-free, but at n = 39 the cofactor is 8,012, so it discarded 8,011 draws in 8,012 and the select stage took 607 seconds. Multiplying by the cofactor made it immeasurable. When a rejection test and a construction agree, take the construction.

The crossover there is in amortising a factor-base-logarithm database over many targets.

Pruning is where the algorithmic content is

A signed orbit is both a column the linear algebra must determine and a set of summands that make targets reachable. The search scores candidates — the divisor lattice of xn − 1 in a dimension window, Frobenius unions of random seeds, 2-torsion saturations — then greedily drops an orbit while that lowers (U + 1 + extra)/pm. Because an exact witness list is kept, each pruning step is a recount rather than a re-search: removing orbit o loses exactly the targets all of whose witnesses use o. On K1/F215 the legacy 31-point base yields no relation at all in 20,000 trials; the search takes a 301-point, five-column saturated union down to 30 points and one column at unchanged coverage, and the end-to-end run to 21 ms.

03 / POINT DECOMPOSITION

Is this point a sum of three base points?

The oracle at the centre of the run, answered four ways.

pair table retained

Every relation is one answer to a single question: given a probe R, are there P1, …, Pm in the factor base with R = Σ Pi? Four implementations answer it, and the tests assert they always agree, target by target.

The four oracles and what each costs per target
OracleCost per targetWhat it is good at
Enumerate|F|m−1 group operationsThe reference every other oracle is cross-checked against
Pair table (meet in the middle)1 lookup at m = 2, |F| at m = 3, |F|² at m = 4What the winning pipeline runs; indifferent to the base's structure
Gröbner / matrix-F4 on Semaevgoverned by the degree of regularity, not by |F|Refutation certificates, and the only one that scales past a materialised base
SAT (CDCL)one conflict per candidate tuple on this encodingA control; it is an enumerator at one further remove

The pair table, and the rule for how much of it to build

The table holds every pair sum Pi + Pj under a packed key that separates P from −P by comparing y with x + y — exact to n ≤ 62 — at |F|(|F|+1)/2 entries of 16 bytes, built once per run. At m = 3 a probe then costs |F| lookups of the rests R − Pk instead of |F|² group operations. In the winning pipeline the table is folded: one witness per Frobenius orbit of a sum, keyed by a normal-basis rotation, and decomposition stops at the first witness it meets.

A full table is not always worth building. The round-0007 rule builds the t rows (1 ≤ t ≤ K) that minimise the expected total of entries built plus rests scanned:

minimise t·|F| + (K + 3) / (λ · cov(t)) with λ = |F|(|F|+1) / 2r, cov(t) = 1 − ((K − t)/K)²

a fixed function of the public base size, column count and subgroup order — no tuning per instance. On these cells it selects one row (n13, n17, n19) or two (n23) where the previous density rule built seven or eight. Probes then scan a few more rests, which is cheaper than the rows they replace. The round-0007 ablation that isolates it, tiny2_rows — the row rule alone on the previous source — measured 0.7667 of rho's instructions and 0.9469 of its native time on development, against 0.8162 and 0.9389 for that round's arithmetic changes alone and 0.7444 and 0.9269 for both together. (An earlier ablation, tiny_fulltable at 0.2785 of its incumbent against 0.2711, tested round 0006's witness-density rule, not this one.)

Probes are walked, not drawn

Collection probes are multiples of the generator alone, Rt = [at]G, advanced by Rt+1 = Rt + S — one point addition per probe rather than one scalar multiplication. A relation is exactly m = 3 base-point indices with Σ Pi = [a]G, repetition allowed, no direct relations; the target only enters at the end, in the single descent relation [a]G + [b]Q = Σ Pi. Whatever the oracle says, the witness is re-added in the group before it becomes a relation, so a wrong answer cannot become a relation.

Why three summands, and when three are impossible

The decomposition rate is pm ≈ |F|m/(m!·r), so m = 3 is what makes a base of a few dozen points reach a subgroup of millions. Whether three summands are admissible at all depends on where the base lives. With a base drawn from ⟨G⟩ every sum stays in ⟨G⟩ and the question never arises, which is why the cofactor-2 cells above run at m = 3. With the older subspace-union bases it does arise: on K1/211, K1/217 and K1/223 three summands are inadmissible because no cofactor-class cancellation is available, and every solver reports that up front instead of searching. The admissibility walk that decides this used to cost |layer| · |classes| ≈ 2.2·106 point additions at h = 1492; generating each sumset layer from one representative per signed orbit and closing it by squaring took a degree-31 run from 68 s to 10 s.

The exponent does not move. Collection needs ≈ 2l relations; a random target decomposes with probability ≈ 23l−n/3!, so it takes 3!·2n−3l targets per relation; each target costs Θ(22l) with any oracle that enumerates the base. The product is Θ(2n), independent of l — a larger base needs fewer tries and makes each try proportionally more expensive, and the two cancel exactly. Rho is 2n/2. The measured projected-collection column is flat within 1.5× across l = 6…10 while 2n grows 4,000-fold, which is the benchmark measuring this rather than asserting it.

04 / SUMMATION POLYNOMIALS

Semaev's polynomials, and what they cost

The algebraic route: the one that scales, and the one that is still slower here.

not in the winning pipeline

Semaev's m-th summation polynomial Sm vanishes exactly when ±P1 ± … ± Pm = O for points with those abscissae. For m = 3 over a binary curve:

S₃(x₁, x₂, x(R)) = (x₁+x₂)² x(R)² + x₁x₂ x(R) + (x₁x₂)² + b = 0

Writing xi = Σt ui,t bt over an F2-basis of the invariant subspace and Weil-restricting turns one F2n-equation into n Boolean equations in m·ℓ unknowns. Restricting the unknowns to a subspace is what makes the system solvable at all — and it is why the algebraic route needs a linear factor base, the shape the measured section above shows is expensive for the rate.

Degree is the whole story

Squaring is F2-linear in characteristic 2, and in the Boolean quotient p² = p, so a symbolic square is a permutation of coordinates and x₁x₂ is bilinear: the m = 2 system is quadratic. For m ≥ 3, S₃ is chained rather than resolved — m summands become m−1 links over m−2 intermediate field unknowns — and because those intermediates are unknown too, the term x₁x₂·e makes the chained system cubic. Sk has degree 2k−2 per variable. That is why m ≥ 3 costs so much more in every engine, algebraic or SAT.

Solving it: matrix-F4, and the refutation it can give

The Macaulay matrix is reduced at degree 2, 3, …; a reduction yielding the constant 1 refutes the branch, rows collapsed to vi (+1) are propagated, and where the algebra stalls the solver splits on the lowest free variable. Roots are lifted back to points — S₃ fixes the summands only up to sign — and the group identity re-checked.

Where the algebra wins is refutation. On K0/F27 the invariant subspace has 8 elements but only one is the abscissa of a curve point, so no target decomposes: all 28 targets are closed by an F4 infeasibility certificate with zero branches searched — an answer exhaustive search cannot give in kind, only by exhausting the space. Where it does not win is wall-clock at these sizes: at |F| = 55 the search is 55 point additions while the Macaulay matrix already has 2,325 columns. The honest statement is that the algebraic oracle is implemented, correct and cross-checked, and it is the one whose cost is governed by the degree of regularity rather than by |F| — but at n ≤ 24 the brute oracle is faster, and the pipeline that beats rho above uses the pair table instead.

Not enumerating triples: S₄ by pairs and a linearised gcd

S₄ is a quartic in its last argument, so fixing X₁ and X₂ leaves a degree-4 polynomial in X₃ whose four roots are the only candidates. Finding which of those roots lie in the factor base is cheap because an F₂-subspace V has a linearised vanishing polynomial — degree 2ℓ but only ℓ+1 non-zero coefficients:

L_V(t) = Π_{v ∈ V} (t + v) = Σ_{i=0}^{ℓ} a_i · t^{2^i} roots of q in V = gcd(q, L_V mod q)

so L_V mod q costs ℓ squarings of a degree-3 polynomial and there is no search over the factor base at all. L_V is built one basis vector at a time from Li+1(t) = Li(t)² + Li(b)·Li(t). Per pair that is O(ℓ) field operations where evaluating over the base would be 2ℓ, so the saving grows like 2ℓ/ℓ — and the measured speedup column doubles per rung, exactly as predicted.

And it changes nothing about whether the attack works: the oracle got roughly a thousand times faster and the attack's exponent did not move. What the speed buys is a usable experiment — ℓ = 12 was a day and a half per target and is now 15 seconds, which is the range where the only thing that would change the conclusion, a sub-22ℓ decomposition oracle, could actually be tested.

Symmetrisation, and what is still missing

Because the summands are interchangeable, rewriting Sm in the elementary symmetric functions e1, …, em reduces density: S₃ from 17 X-monomials to at most 10, S₄ by about 5×, and for S₈ over a degree-5 extension by about 100× — the difference between memory-bound and computable. The first of those reductions is verified in CI here; the S₅ and S₈ symmetrisations, the Newton-identity change of variables and the block-Vieta variables are not yet implemented. The same symmetry is why the SAT oracle refutes each root m! times; adding exact lexicographic symmetry-breaking constraints changed no verdict and raised conflict counts (5,512 → 6,412 on K0/29 at m = 3), so it stays off by default as a control.

10 / READING THE RESULTS

What the numbers mean

Less time vs. speedup

Using 44.6% of reference time means about 55.4% less time, or 2.24× the local throughput for the same work. It does not mean the full solver is 2.24× faster.

Measured work saved

The example specialization uses 143 fewer field multiplications. Correcting the old estimate from 1,770 to 1,342 does not count as work saved.

Why the interval matters

An interval crossing 100% includes no change. All full-DLP confirmation intervals do, so their point estimates alone cannot establish a gain.

Measurement boundaries and sources

Local comparisons come from the six-variant summation-polynomial ablation: 342 runs, including 589,824 timed specializations, 6,480 verified residual solves and 90 verified cold full-DLP runs. A further 140 full-DLP runs form the confirmation. All 15 Gaudry unit tests pass.

Intervals bootstrap entire input clusters, keeping repetitions together. Local benchmarks have eight curve/seed clusters; confirmation has seven. Timings come from a shared host. Full-DLP processes include cold setup, verification, output, and independent MITM/rho auditing.

Complete common-operation S, cost/rho and cost/floor remain unmeasured. Polynomial degree, relation yield and asymptotic boundaries are unchanged. Full-DLP times do not describe a production deployment.

The rho comparison in section 01 is a different corpus from the summation-polynomial measurements: 1,440 profiled trials in round 0013 alone, each with a paired native run, and every ratio taken against a fresh matched rho run in the same round, 180 verified complete-DLP runs per arm on confirmation and 180 on replay, across five Koblitz cells. Every job's logarithm is certified by its descent relation and independently audited. Only within-round ratios are claimed; absolute instruction counts and times are not comparable across rounds, because the executable, the clock and the shared baseline changed.
