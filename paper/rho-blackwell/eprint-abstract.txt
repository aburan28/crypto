We measure what Pollard rho costs on the current generation of NVIDIA hardware, using the Certicom challenge ECC2K-130 as the yardstick. ECC2K-130 is the Koblitz curve $y^2+xy=x^3+1$ over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{131}}$, open since 1997, with an expected cost of $2^{60.9}$ iterations of a Frobenius-class rho. In 2010 the best published GPU kernel ran at $0.064$ billion iterations per second on a GTX 295 card. Rebuilt around the carry-less multiply that CUDA 13.3 exposes in PTX (clmad), three-input logic and a packed normal-basis representation, the same walk now runs at $14.64$ billion iterations per second on one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell in its audited campaign build and $15.88$ in the best later build, about $230$ times the 2010 card, so the whole budget is about five GPU-years. We trace that gain through sixteen paired optimisation steps and measure clmad on three dies: its issue rate per SM-clock differs by up to $18\times$ between the workstation and data-centre parts, so the same change can win on one Blackwell part and lose on another. We report twelve GPUs, Turing to Blackwell, per card, per SM and per dollar, show the kernel and where its speedup came from (none of it from changing the algorithm), and set the work against the instruction sets that made it possible on GPUs and CPUs. We separate the $\sigma$-walk from the table walk: the table walk's $20.08$ was measured under a cycle rule later found to leave cycles inconsistently, the exact rule runs at $8.24$ today, and the $\sigma$-walk is the fastest correct walk we have. Applying the same optimisations to Pollard rho on NIST P-256 and P-384 on the same card gives $1.52\times$ and $2.61\times$, to $1.632$ and $0.548$ billion iterations per second; the levers transfer, but their relative sizes do not. Upper bounds derived from measured pipe rates put any one-addition walk on one RTX PRO 6000 at about $24$–$26$ billion iterations per second. In calendar terms, a fleet of five RTX PRO 6000s expects to solve ECC2K-130 in a year, 56 in a month and a thousand in about three days, where the 2010 kernel needed over a thousand cards for a one-year solve; at the same rates P-256 remains about $6\cdot10^{21}$ card-years away. The walk constant is measured rather than assumed, at $2^{60.91}$–$2^{60.92}$ iterations, and a floor derived from the carry-less unit before tuning rules out $28$ billion iterations per second on one card. We also report an FPGA engine at $8.25$ billion iterations per second on one AWS F2; a campaign that stored $486$ million distinguished points and $2^{56.68}$ iterations ($5.4\%$ of the expected work) before its fleet was reclaimed; and the open questions we would pursue next. Every number cites an evidence record; the code, data and a Docker image that starts the ECC2K-130 walk are being prepared for release with the paper. None of this improves on the generic-group bound, and we do not claim that it does.
