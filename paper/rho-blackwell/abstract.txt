We show how far Pollard rho can be accelerated on current NVIDIA GPUs by
rebuilding its field arithmetic around the hardware, with the Certicom
challenge ECC2K-130, the Koblitz curve $y^2+xy=x^3+1$ over
$\FF_{2^{131}}$ with an expected cost of $2^{60.9}$ iterations, as the
benchmark. None of the speedup comes from changing the algorithm. It
comes from a packed normal-basis representation in which every
Frobenius power is a permutation network instead of a chain of
squarings; from the carry-less multiply that CUDA~13.3 exposes in PTX
(\texttt{clmad}), which forms a 131-bit product in six instructions;
from three-input logic instructions for everything around it; and from
a batch size, launch geometry and compact tiled state chosen by
measurement against the pipes they load. Across sixteen paired steps,
each held to a rule written down before it ran, these take one
RTX~PRO~6000 Blackwell from a $0.85$ billion iterations per second
bitsliced control to $14.64$ in the audited campaign build, a
$17.2\times$ gain (representation and arithmetic $6.3\times$, geometry
$1.5\times$, memory layout $1.3\times$, \texttt{clmad} $1.2\times$), and a
later build reaches $15.88$. That is about $230$ times the best published
2010 GPU kernel per card and puts the whole challenge under five
GPU-years: five cards expect a solution within a year, a thousand in
about three days. The gains depend on the die. \texttt{clmad} issues ten
to eighteen times faster per SM-clock on the data-centre parts than on
the workstation part, so the same change can win on one Blackwell part
and lose on another; we report twelve GPUs from Turing to Blackwell per
card, per SM and per dollar, and the CPU instruction sets that play the
same role. Upper bounds derived from measured pipe rates leave about
$1.5\times$ of headroom on one RTX~PRO~6000, at $24$--$26$ billion
iterations per second for any walk with one addition per step. The same
levers applied to Pollard rho on NIST P-256 and P-384 on the same card
give $1.52\times$ and $2.61\times$, to $1.632$ and $0.548$ billion
iterations per second, which still leaves P-256 about $6\cdot10^{21}$
card-years away. We also separate the $\sigma$-walk from a table walk
whose $20.08$ was measured under a cycle rule later found unsound (the
exact rule runs at $8.24$), measure the walk constant at
$2^{60.91}$--$2^{60.92}$ iterations, and report an FPGA engine at $8.25$
billion iterations per second on one AWS F2 and a campaign that walked
$2^{56.68}$ iterations, $5.4\%$ of the expected work. Every number cites
an evidence record; the code, data, evidence and a Dockerfile whose
image starts the ECC2K-130 walk are at
https://github.com/aburan28/rho-blackwell. None of this improves on the
generic-group bound, and we do not claim that it does.
