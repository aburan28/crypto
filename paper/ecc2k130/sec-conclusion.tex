\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We can summarise what this work established in four points.

First, ECC2K-130 is an arithmetic problem at a known exponent, and
nothing we tried moved the exponent. Index calculus on the curve,
priced end to end with four-summand relations, costs $10^7$ times as
much as rho at the challenge size. The best measured decomposition is
$35$--$51$ bits short of its per-call budget, and the
Frobenius-stable subspaces that the standard construction needs do not
exist at $n=131$. The target is still $\sqrt{\pi\ell/(2\cdot262)}$
class steps.

Second, we measured the cost of a GPU step against a floor we wrote
down first. Sixteen paired steps took one RTX~PRO~6000 from $0.85$ to
$14.64$ billion complete iterations per second on the campaign's
$\sigma$-walk; later $\sigma$-walk builds reached $15.88$, and a table
walk reached $20.08$, as a rate. The roofline of that build has the
logic pipe $91\%$ busy and the carry-less unit $73\%$. For any walk that
performs one affine addition per step, $28$~B/s on one card is out of
reach on both pipes, and $30$ needs two cards. Our own $17.0$~B/s
target, declared in advance, rejected a $16.56$~B/s result, which is
exactly what a declared target is for.

The same ladder carried over to prime fields better than we expected,
though not in the same proportions. On NIST P-256 and P-384, on the
same card, explicit carry chains, a wider shared inversion and the
same refusal of register-cap trades took the rho walk from $1.075$ to
$1.632$~G steps/s and from $0.210$ to $0.548$, gains of $1.52\times$ and
$2.61\times$. The batch width, a modest knob on the binary walk, was
the biggest lever on the prime one, because a Fermat inversion is so
much more expensive there. The rest of the GPU arithmetic in the
repositories is verified and statically accounted to the same standard
but, apart from F4 elimination on rented cards, has not yet run on a
device, and we have not quoted its emulations as rates.

Third, the step count is measured, not assumed. The campaign's
$\sigma$-walk needs $2^{60.91}$--$2^{60.92}$ iterations, which agrees
with the 2009 estimate to its own precision. The table walk's cheaper
constant was paid back several times over by fruitless cycles until
the cycle rule was fixed, and its projected $0.81$--$0.86$ relative
cost per solve still awaits the paired run that would confirm it. An
FPGA engine built from the same model measured $8.25$~G steps/s on one
F2, against a pre-board central estimate of $8$, and is level with the
GPU on cost per solve.

Fourth, the campaign's output is a corpus we can stand behind. It
holds one report per $2^{28.41}$ iterations at cutoff 32, a figure that
replaced an earlier, wrong $2^{27.9}$, and we say so. It contains $486$
million points and $2^{56.68}$ iterations with no collision, $5.4\%$ of
the expected work. Every record can be re-walked from its seed, and
every rate in this paper was re-walked before it was recorded. The
fleet that produced most of it was reclaimed on 2026-09-18, the last
point landed on 2026-10-07, and collection is paused. Whether the
remaining $94.6\%$ ever gets walked is a question of money, not
mathematics. What this paper contributes is an honest price.
